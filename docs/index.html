Why the surveys, not the estimators

Most graduate training inverts the priority. Students learn fixed effects and GMM in some detail, but rarely learn how a survey is designed, where it breaks, and what its structure does and does not let them identify. Yet the design choices — multistage sampling, rotating and refreshment panels, attrition, the household that splits between rounds — are exactly what determine whether a clean-looking regression means anything.

A careful analyst needs both halves, and the second is the one usually missing. The workshop follows Deaton's progression from design to description to demand to dynamics, working throughout with data from the region: Ghana's Living Standards Surveys as the running local example, Uganda's National Panel Survey for the panel and demand material, and comparisons across the LSMS countries where they earn their place.

The six sessions

  1. Design, sampling, and collection

    What to know before touching the data: population, frame, weights. Two-stage cluster sampling and stratification, worked through on Ghana's GLSS7 and a regional sample from Guinea-Bissau — reading a design back out of the weights it produced, and watching an unweighted population pyramid misstate the number of children.

  2. Describing welfare

    The theory the measurement rests on. What object a consumption aggregate is standing in for, the household's dynamic problem and the marginal utility of money as a welfare index, and an explicit accounting of the assumptions — homotheticity, cardinalisation, separable durables — that dividing expenditure by a price index quietly buys.

  3. Elicitation of consumption and survey design

    How the number in the aggregate is actually produced: item lists, recall periods and diaries, own production and how to price it, durables and imputed rent, spatial deflation, equivalence scales. Then poverty and inequality on Ghana — FGT, Lorenz, Gini, Atkinson — weighted and unweighted, across four rounds.

  4. Demand and welfare

    Engel curves and the Working–Leser form on GLSS7; unit values and the quality correction; the rank of a demand system, and why rank above one means no single price index will do. Then a constant-Frisch-elasticity demand system fitted to Uganda's eight waves, which needs neither prices nor total expenditure, and the exact welfare cost of a price change rather than the first-order approximation.

  5. From cross-sections to panels

    Fixed effects and two-way fixed effects as a generalised double difference, strict exogeneity, attrition, and clustered inference. Worked on Ghana's GLSS1–2 farm panel, where Mundlak's management bias is visible in the data, and on Uganda's NPS; then pseudo-panels from repeated cross-sections, for when there is no panel to be had.

  6. Capstone and frontiers — online

    Where the methods break down: a full risk-sharing test on Uganda and why it has power only against idiosyncratic shocks, Nickell bias in short dynamic panels, non-classical measurement error, and endogeneity. The capstone takes a survey of your own choosing through a small analysis that runs from a clean kernel.

Working with this afterwards

The installation instructions assume no prior Python and take you through it a step at a time. It is roughly a 1.3 GB download, so do it on a connection you trust. Installed locally, the library downloads the survey data itself; the sessions read pre-built extracts only because that was faster in a classroom.

Sessions 4 to 6 also import datamat, which is not pulled in as a dependency, so install it alongside: pip install LSMS_Library datamat jupyterlab.

If something does not work, please say so. Open an issue at ligon/hhsurveys for anything about the workshop materials, or at ligon/LSMS_Library for the library itself — a bad extract, a country that will not load, a variable that is not what it claims. Either is more useful than working around it quietly: the library is a work in progress and these reports are how it improves.

A World Bank Microdata account was not needed during the workshop and is still not required, but it is free and worth having for your own research; the installation page explains where the API key goes.

Reading

  • Angus Deaton, The Analysis of Household Surveys: A Microeconometric Approach to Development Policy (Johns Hopkins, 1997) is the spine. The World Bank's 2018 reissue is open access, so it costs you nothing: full PDF (landing page). Chapter 1 is a good place to start.
  • Deaton and Zaidi, Guidelines for Constructing Consumption Aggregates for Welfare Analysis (World Bank LSMS Working Paper 135, 2002) — the reference for sessions 2 and 3.
  • Beegle, De Weerdt, Friedman and Gibson (2012), “Methods of household consumption measurement through surveys: experimental results from Tanzania,” Journal of Development Economics — how much of measured poverty is the questionnaire. Session 3.
  • Deaton and Muellbauer (1980), “An Almost Ideal Demand System,” American Economic Review 70:312–326, and Lewbel (1991), “The rank of demand systems,” Econometrica 59:711–730. Session 4.
  • Ligon, “Estimating Household Welfare from Disaggregate Expenditures” (CUDARE working paper, 2020) — the constant-Frisch-elasticity system estimated in session 4, and open access at eScholarship.
  • Deaton (1985), “Panel data from time series of cross-sections,” Journal of Econometrics — the origin of the pseudo-panel methods in session 5; with Mundlak (1961) on management bias and Abadie, Athey, Imbens and Wooldridge (2023) on when to cluster.
  • Townsend (1994), “Risk and insurance in village India,” Econometrica, and Ligon (2026), “Risk sharing tests and covariate shocks: drought, floods, and pests in Uganda,” American Economic Review, forthcoming. Session 6.

Software and data

The hands-on work uses LSMS_Library, an open-source Python package giving a uniform interface to the household surveys of roughly forty countries, together with CFEDemands for the demand system in sessions 4 to 6 and DataMat for labelled linear algebra. All three are under active development, and the workshop was taught against LSMS_Library 0.14.0 and CFEDemands 0.10.0.

World Bank microdata cannot be freely redistributed, so the sessions worked from harmonised extracts prepared in advance. The materials repository contains code and text only — no survey data.
